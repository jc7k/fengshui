# How Practitioners Reconcile the Schools

> Read this before [[Module-Guide|the Capstone]]. Your conflict table is about to compute, in a
> few dozen lines of Python, a problem that real practitioners have argued about for centuries —
> and it will help to know how they argue it.

## Why it matters
The capstone refuses to average the schools into one score. You might worry that's a cop-out. It
isn't: real practitioners refuse too, each in their own way. Nobody in the tradition runs four
schools and takes the mean. What they actually do — weight one school first, inherit a stack from
a teacher, or ignore rival methods entirely — is this note's subject. When your conflict table
prints its first DISAGREE flag, you'll know what a human expert would do with it.

## The classical split: form and compass
You've met the two great branches in
[[../01-Feng-Shui-Fundamentals/Schools-Overview|Schools Overview]]; here are the umbrella terms
the tradition itself uses, because you'll meet them constantly in real books:

- **巒頭 (luán tóu)** — literally "mountain peaks": the **form** branch. Everything you can see —
  landforms, water, roads, room shapes, sight lines. Your Week 5 Form School work lives here.
- **理氣 (lǐ qì)** — roughly "principle qi" or "regulating qi": the **compass-methods** branch.
  Everything you *can't* see — directional and time-based energy, read with the luopan. This is
  the umbrella over **Ba Zhai** (Week 3), **Xuan Kong Fei Xing** (Week 4), and much more.

Within 理氣 there's a second, older split: the **三合 (sān hé, "Three Harmonies")** camp, whose
methods key mostly off fixed directions and the surrounding landscape, versus the **三元 (sān
yuán, "Three Cycles")** camp, whose methods — Xuan Kong Flying Stars chief among them — put
*time* at the center. The camps use different luopan rings and formulas, and have spent centuries
politely (and not so politely) disputing each other.

Here's the part that reframes everything: for most classical masters, the real reconciliation
mechanism isn't intellectual at all. It's **lineage** — 派 (pài), "school" or "branch." A master
doesn't survey all the schools and assemble a personal best-of. They **inherit a stack** from
their teacher, who inherited it from theirs. A San Yuan practitioner flies stars because that's
what their 派 does; the San He methods down the road are simply someone else's tradition. Most
masters don't reconcile the schools — they were *handed* a reconciliation, pre-made, and loyalty
to it is part of the craft.

## How working practitioners stack them
Within a lineage, though, there *is* a common working hierarchy, and it's remarkably consistent
across camps:

1. **Form first.** There's a traditional maxim you'll hear quoted as "**70% luán tóu, 30% lǐ
   qì**" — the visible environment dominates, the calculations refine. Be honest about what that
   number is: a *maxim*, not a measurement. Nobody derived it; some sources say 70/30, others
   "at least half," others just "forms supersede formulas." But the *ordering* it encodes is
   near-universal among classical practitioners: a beautiful flying-star chart can't rescue a
   house facing a cliff, so you read the land before you unpack the luopan.
2. **The lineage's primary compass method second.** A Xuan Kong specialist charts the stars; a
   San He practitioner works their direction formulas. This is the main calculated layer.
3. **Other methods consulted, not obeyed.** Many practitioners *check* Ba Zhai for the occupants
   or glance at a rival method — agreement is reassurance, disagreement a reason to look harder —
   but the primary method keeps the deciding vote.

**BTB** sits outside this whole structure. It's a separate modern stream (Week 6's story) that
rarely mixes with classical methods — a BTB consultant doesn't rank the compass schools below
form, they simply don't use a compass. That conflict isn't adjudicated in practice; the two
worlds mostly just don't attend each other's conferences.

## Worked example: one sector, three verdicts
Make it concrete. Take a home with a **south-facing front door**, and stand in its **Southeast
sector**:

- **Compass bagua says: Wealth.** Southeast is the Wealth direction, always.
- **BTB says: Helpful People.** Lay the three-door bagua on the south door wall and the
  Southeast corner sits by the door on the right — Helpful People. BTB's Wealth has moved to the
  far-left of the door: the *Northwest*.
- **The natal chart says: careful.** Suppose it drops a **5 star** into the Southeast palace —
  the one star every lineage treats with caution (and the one our own code flags as its shakiest
  rule).

Now the verdicts. A **form-first classical master** barely glances at any of that until they've
read the room: if the Southeast is a cramped corner behind a poison-arrow corridor, no label will
redeem it; if it's bright and well-proportioned, their compass method then refines the reading —
and takes the 5 star seriously, because their lineage says so. A **Flying Stars specialist**
leads with the chart: 5 in the Southeast means keep it quiet, don't renovate there this cycle,
whatever the bagua label claims. A **BTB consultant** never measures a direction at all: this is
your Helpful People area — and go activate Wealth in the Northwest.

Same room, three confident, mutually incompatible verdicts — and the punchline: **none of them is
wrong by their own system's rules.** Each answer follows perfectly from its premises; the
premises just differ. That's not sloppiness, it's what a family of traditions *is*. Your conflict
table is exactly this scene, with the faces removed and the logic kept.

## What a professional consultation looks like
So what do you actually get if you hire a 風水師? A classical consultation usually runs:

1. **Intake.** Birth data for every occupant (year at minimum — this feeds Kua numbers and, in
   fuller practice, [[../09-Bonus-BaZi/Four-Pillars-Ba-Zi|Ba Zi]]), the construction year, and a
   floor plan with north marked.
2. **Exterior survey.** The 巒頭 pass: landforms, neighbors, roads, water — before entering the
   house. Form first, remember.
3. **Luopan readings at the door.** *Multiple* readings, averaged, declination-corrected, taken
   away from steel and wiring. (Your Week 4 module refuses boundary facings for exactly the
   reason a careful practitioner re-measures them.)
4. **Floor-plan work.** Overlaying the practitioner's methods on the plan, room by room.
5. **A written report.** Typically: the facing measurement and how it was taken, the charts
   (flying-star grid, Kua directions), sector-by-sector findings, and ranked recommendations.
6. **Follow-up** after changes are made.

Reading such a report as a homeowner, look for the virtues your capstone has: does it *show its
measurements*? Does it say which school each claim comes from? Does it distinguish "my lineage
holds" from "all schools agree"?

⚠️ One honest consumer paragraph, because this field has a real fringe: walk away from
**fear-selling** ("your house will harm your family unless…"), from **mandatory expensive
cures** — a consultant whose every finding is solved by an object they happen to sell — and from
**guaranteed outcomes** ("wealth within a year"). Reputable practitioners explain their method,
name their lineage, and leave you informed rather than frightened. A tradition this old deserves
better salesmen than that.

## The metaphor 🏠
You get three contractors' quotes on the same house. One inspected the foundation, one the
wiring, one the layout. Of course the quotes disagree — they looked at different systems. Your
job as the homeowner isn't to average the dollar amounts into one meaningless number; it's to
understand *what each contractor looked at*, then decide, as the person who has to live there.
Your conflict table is the three quotes stapled together, with a sticky note on every page where
they contradict.

## How we compute it in code
`src/report.py` is this note made executable. `report.full_report(home, occupants)` runs every
school and returns their sections side by side; `rep['conflict_table']` holds one row per sector
with the compass life area, the BTB life area, a `conflict` flag where they differ, plus that
sector's flying stars and each occupant's Eight Mansions quality. And note what is *absent*: no
combined score, no winner field, no weighted average. That absence is the design — present each
system faithfully, mark the disagreements, and leave the deciding vote to a human.

## Talk it through
- With a practitioner: "When your form reading and your flying-star chart disagree about the
  same room, which wins, and why? Is that your judgment, or your lineage's rule?"
- For yourself: before you run the capstone on your own home, predict which sectors will
  disagree. (Hint from Week 6: unless your front door faces north, the BTB and compass maps
  *must* diverge somewhere.) Write the prediction down, then check it against the table.
